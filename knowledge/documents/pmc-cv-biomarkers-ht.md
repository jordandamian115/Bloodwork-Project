Abstract
Objective:
To assess the long-term changes in cardiovascular biomarkers during the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) hormone therapy (HT) clinical trials of conjugated equine estrogens (CEE) alone and CEE plus medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) . Methods:
HT trial participants from the CEE alone (n=1,188, 0.625 mg/day of CEE or placebo) and the CEE + MPA trials (n=1,508, 0.625 mg/day of CEE plus continuous 2.5 mg/day of MPA or placebo) provided blood samples at baseline and after 1, 3, and 6 years. Low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C; primary endpoint), high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, total cholesterol, lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), glucose, insulin, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) were measured. Repeated-measures regression models estimated the geometric means of each log-transformed biomarker by restricted maximum likelihood. A constant treatment effect across visits was used to estimate the overall effect, expressed as a ratio of geometric means, and was complemented with geometric means (95% confidence interval (CI)) by randomization group, and corresponding ratios of geometric means (rGM, 95%CI; HT vs. placebo) at each visit. Results:
During the intervention phase of CEE alone trial, randomization to CEE reduced LDL-C by 11% over 6 years; rGM (95%CI) = 0.89 (0.88–0.91), P < 0.001. The overall reduction in LDL-C was similar for CEE+MPA, relative to placebo; rGM (95%CI) = 0.88 (0.86–0.89), P < 0.001. Relative to placebo, HDL-C and triglycerides were 13.0% and 7.0% higher with CEE and CEE + MPA, respectively. HOMA-IR decreased by 14.0% and 8.0% for CEE-alone and CEE+MPA trial participants, respectively. Relative to placebo, Lp(a) decreased by 15.0% and 20.0% for participants randomized to CEE-alone and CEE+MPA, respectively. Conclusion:
LDL-C, Lp(a) and HOMA-IR were lower and HDL levels were higher, comparing the HT to placebo.. Triglycerides increased in both CEE and CEE + MPA trials, however.. Future research should assess whether other progestogens attenuate estrogen’s effect on HDL-C. These results may be used to counsel younger menopausal women with bothersome symptoms who are deciding whether to initiate oral HT within the context of published effects of oral HT on rates of cardiovascular events. Clinical Trial Registration:
ClinicalTrials.gov , NCT00000611 . Keywords: Hormone therapy, conjugated equine estrogens, medroxyprogesterone, menopause, cardiovascular biomarkers, cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, insulin, glucose Precis:
Oral hormone therapy was associated with beneficial long-term effects on cardiovascular biomarkers. Introduction:
Prior studies examined the effect of hormone therapy (HT) on cardiovascular disease (CVD) biomarkers which may influence the risk of cardiovascular events in menopausal women. 1 – 3 The Postmenopausal Estrogen/Progestin Interventions Trial randomly assigned 875 healthy postmenopausal women to one of five groups: a placebo group, a group receiving conjugated equine estrogen (CEE) alone, and three groups receiving CEE combined with different progestin regimens; details have been previously described. After three years, all formulations decreased low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) which has been shown to reduce CVD risk 4 and increased triglycerides which is associated CVD risk in women. 5 Compared to placebo, increases in high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C) were higher in the CEE alone arm than in the CEE plus continuous MPA arm. 1
In the WHI-estrogen plus progestin trial (CEE + MPA), short-term results from baseline to year one showed that CEE + MPA decreased total cholesterol, LDL-C, glucose, and insulin and increased HDL-C and triglycerides. 6 Similar findings were observed in the WHI-estrogen alone (CEE) trial, for LDL-C, HDL-C and triglycerides from baseline to year 1. 7 Cross sectional comparisons at 3 years (CEE+MPA) and 6 years (CEE) among nondiabetic participants have been conducted, 8 , 9 however, the long-term effects of HT on CVD biomarkers have not been longitudinally analyzed, or stratified by age group. Therefore, our study aims to analyze the effects of CEE alone and CEE + MPA on CVD biomarkers during the intervention phase, spanning approximately six years of follow-up. Methods:
The two double-blinded, placebo-controlled WHI-HT trials randomized 27,347 participants. Randomization was done using a permuted block algorithm, stratified by trial, age group, and clinical center, with a 1:1 allocation and block sizes of 5, 10, or 15. This algorithm was developed at the coordinating center and implemented locally through a distributed study database. Unique bottle numbers and barcodes on study medication bottles ensured blinded dispensing, keeping both clinic personnel and participants unaware of individual treatment assignments. The trials included postmenopausal women ages 50–79 years at randomization with coronary heart disease as the primary outcome. Of these, 10,739 women had a prior hysterectomy and were in the estrogen-alone trial (0.625 mg/day of CEE or daily placebo tablet), 10 , 11 and 16,608 women who had an intact uterus were in the estrogen plus progestin trial (0.625 mg/day of CEE plus continuous 2.5 mg/day of medroxyprogesterone (MPA) or placebo). 6 Institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained at each clinical center and all participants in this study provided written informed consent.
All trial participants provided a fasting blood sample at baseline and year 1. A subsample of trial participants were randomly selected at baseline to provide additional blood samples at years 3, 6, and 9. Specifically, 2,696 of 27,347 (~10%) trial participants were selected via stratified random sampling with strata determined by geographically disperse clinical centers (n=40), hysterectomy status, age group, and 6-fixed race and ethnicity categories (self-reported); odds for selection were at least 6-fold higher than for non-Hispanic White women ( Figure 1 ). The trial’s sampling design intended for considerable precision in estimating HT effects for Black and Hispanic women, with some precision for American Indian and Asian women, 10 so these racial/ethnic categories were used for statistical analyses. Cross-tabulations with new self-identified race and ethnicity data, collected in 2003 from active participants using US 2000 Census categories, are shown in Appendix 1 & 2 . 12

HT, hormone therapy; CEE, conjugated equine estrogens; MPA, medroxyprogesterone acetate. Specimens were analyzed for apolipoprotein lipids, micronutrients, clotting factors, insulin, and glucose concentrations at baseline, years 1, 3, and 6. Blood samples were collected and processed, then stored in a central biorepository at −80°C. Analyses were conducted at a central laboratory in single batches, with 10.0% blind duplicates within eight years of collection. Total cholesterol, HDL, triglyceride, Lp(a), glucose, and insulin concentrations were measured in a standardized manner with processes that have been described previously. 13 The Friedewald formula was used to calculate LDL-C for those with a triglyceride value below 400 mg/dL. The primary endpoint of this study was LDL-C. Secondary endpoints (pre-specified) included total cholesterol, HDL-C, triglycerides, Lp(a), glucose, insulin, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Factor VII activity, factor VII antigen, and fibrinogen were included as exploratory biomarkers as they have been shown to associate with stroke and were suggested after internal peer review. 14 , 15 Systolic blood pressure and waist circumference were included as contextual markers.
Repeated-measures regression models estimated mean concentrations of each log-transformed biomarker by restricted maximum likelihood. 16 Within-subject variability was modeled with an unstructured variance-covariance matrix, as biomarker concentrations were more strongly correlated between temporally closer visits (e.g., year one versus 3) than further apart (e.g., year one versus 6). Older participants and minority women took longer to recruit, 17 , so they were less likely to have biomarkers measured at year 6. Consequently, age and race/ethnicity were included as covariates so that corresponding missing data could be assumed to be missing at random (MAR); likelihood-based methods provide valid inferences when data are MAR. 18 Regression models also included indicator variables for post-randomization visits, interaction terms between the year of visit x randomization group, and a common intercept for baseline, which is an appropriate, efficient model for randomized trials. 18 In the absence of statistically significant qualitative differences in treatment effects between visits (P-interaction ≤ 0.05), an overall constant treatment effect (ratio of geometric means, 95%CI) summarized the long-term effects. When comparing randomization groups, ratios of geometric means are interpreted as: a ratio of 1.00 indicates no difference between groups, while a ratio of 1.20 (or 0.80), for example, corresponds to a 20.0% higher (or lower) mean biomarker concentration in the HT group versus placebo. 19 An overall constant treatment effect allowed for a parsimonious and precise summary for each biomarker, which was extended to subgroup analyses by age groups (50–59, 60–69, 70–79 years), racial and ethnic groups ( Figure 1 ), vasomotor symptom severity (none, mild, moderate or severe), baseline body mass index and baseline hyperlipidemia. Examining the effect of HT in subgroups of women defined by age and race/ethnicity were protocol-specified objectives in the WHI https://www.whi.org/doc/Protocol-1993-2005.pdf .
Multivariate longitudinal analyses 18 compared the effect of CEE+MPA vs CEE-alone between HT trials by estimating the ratio of the mean ratios. Analyses were limited to the first three years of follow-up to account for the earlier stopping of the CEE+MPA trial.
Sensitivity analyses addressed the potential for post-randomization confounding of LDL-C (primary endpoint) due to post-randomization statin use, as statin uptake was greater in the placebo arm of both HT trials, 20 using the reasonable constant method. 21 . Additional sensitivity analysis, which did not assume a constant treatment effect, computed the overall effect by taking a weighted average of the visit-specific rGMs. 22 Adherence analyses for LDL-C (primary endpoint) censored measurements of participants who, based on concurrent assessments, had stopped study pills, taken less than 80% of study pills, or were taking non-study HT. All analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc.) and figures constructed with R (version 4.1; R Core Team). Results:
Within each trial, participant characteristics of the biomarker cohort were balanced between arms, although characteristics differed for each trial ( Table 1 ). In particular, the CEE +MPA cohort had a higher proportion of non-Hispanic Whites and the CEE alone trial a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia than the CEE+MPA cohort.

Because the interventions were stopped earlier than planned, on July 7, 2002, for the CEE+MPA trial and February 29, 2004, for the CEE-alone trial, the length of follow-up for biomarker assessment had a median (Q1–Q3) of 3.0 (3.0–3.0) years for the CEE+MPA trial and 6.0 (3.0–6.0) years for the CEE-alone trial, respectively.
One year after randomization to CEE-alone, there was an 11.0% reduction in LDL-C among women randomized to CEE-alone compared to placebo, with a ratio of geometric means (rGM; 95%CI) of 0.89 (0.86–0.91) ( Appendix 3 , left panel). The effect was mostly persistent at years-3 and 6 (P-value for heterogeneity = 0.09), with rGM (95%CI) values of 0.89 (0.87–0.92) and 0.93 (0.89–0.96), respectively. Overall, there was an 11.0% reduction during the intervention phase, with a rGM (95%CI) of 0.89 (0.88–0.91), P < 0.001.
The overall reduction in LDL-C for CEE+MPA relative to placebo was similar; with a rGM (95%CI) of 0.88 (0.86–0.89), P < 0.001 ( Appendix 3 , right panel). However, the magnitude of the difference in LDL-C appeared to diminish over the follow-up period (P-het < 0.001), with the rGM (95%CI) rising from 0.87 (0.86–0.89) at year-1 to 0.95 (0.91–0.99) by year-6, a period during which study pill adherence declined and statin use increased. Sensitivity analysis, which did not assume a constant effect for CEE+MPA during the intervention phase but instead computed the overall effect by taking a weighted average of the visit-specific rGMs, yielded essentially the same result, with an average rGM (95%CI) of 0.88 (0.86–0.90).
Except for triglycerides, CEE-alone had a favorable influence on all prespecified CVD-biomarkers ( Figure 2 ). Likewise, CEE+MPA also had a favorable effect on all prespecified CVD-biomarkers, except for triglycerides ( Figure 2 , bottom). Relative to placebo, HDL-C was increased by 13.0% and 7.0% for participants randomized to CEE-alone and CEE+MPA, respectively ( Appendix 4 , Figure 2 ). Similar increases of 13.0% and 7.0% were observed for triglycerides in each trial, respectively, resulting in a modest decrease in total cholesterol in the CEE-alone trial and a more significant decrease in the CEE+MPA trial ( Figure 2 , Appendix 5 ). Mean trajectories for the remaining CVD biomarkers are shown in Appendix 6 to 10 . Regarding exploratory markers ( Appendix 11 to 13 ), both CEE and CEE+MPA increased factor VII antigen concentrations (14.0% and 8.0%, respectively). In the CEE alone trial, increases in factor VII antigen were most pronounced at year 1 (16.0% increase) and attenuated later in follow-up. A similar trend was observed for CEE+MPA. CEE increased factor VII coagulant activity (7.0% increase) while CEE+MPA resulted in no increase in factor VII coagulant activity. Fibrinogen concentrations decreased 4.0% with both CEE and CEE+MPA. The geometric means (95%CI) shown in the longitudinal plots are tabularly summarized in Appendix 14 .

Mostly consistent patterns were observed for both trials across racial and ethnic groups, although estimates varied for the smaller subgroups (n < 50; Appendix 15 ). The significant reduction in Lp(a) in the CEE-alone trial was more pronounced among women self-identifying as American Indian or Alaska Native, with rGM (95%CI) = 0.59 (0.42–0.83), and among those identifying as Asian or Pacific Islander, with rGM (95%CI) = 0.62 (0.48–0.78) (P-interaction = 0.004). In the CEE+MPA trial, a similarly large reduction in Lp(a) was also observed for these groups, with rGM (95%CI) = 0.64 (0.45–0.90) and rGM (95%CI) = 0.65 (0.56–0.76), respectively (P-interaction = 0.01). Distributions of Lp(a) at baseline differed by racial and ethnic groups, with concentrations approximately double for Black women ( Appendix16 ). Somewhat more significant reductions in LDL-C were also observed for women self-identifying as American Indian or Alaska Natives and for those identifying as Asian or Pacific Islander in the CEE-alone trial (P-int = 0.05), but not in the CEE+MPA trial (P-int = 0.40).
In addition, similar patterns were observed in subgroups classified by vasomotor symptoms at baseline (none, mild, moderate/severe), with no evidence of effect modification (all P-values for trend > 0.05; Appendix 17 ). Similar patterns were seen across baseline hyperlipidemia subgroups. While significant effect modification was detected for insulin (P-int = 0.03 for CEE-alone) and Lp(a) (P-int = 0.03 for CEE+MPA), these findings were not consistent between trials ( Appendix 18 ). Likewise, similar patterns were observed by baseline BMI group ( Appendix19 ).
Differences by HT status restricted to three years of follow-up, to address differential duration of the interventions, provided nearly the same results as for the entire intervention phase ( Figure 4 ). There were differences between trials (P < 0.001). CEE-alone (vs. placebo) had a more favorable influence on HDL-C than CEE+MPA (vs placebo), but this was offset by a less favorable effect on triglycerides. For example, after three years of intervention, mean triglyceride concentrations increased by 8.0% for CEE+MPA vs 14.0% for CEE-alone, with a ratio of rGMs(95%CI) = 0.95 (0.91, 0.99). Results were similar when adjusted for baseline BMI; ( Appendix20 ).

Statin use more than tripled during the intervention phase, increasing from 7.2% and 6.6% at baseline to 23.3% and 20.7% at year 6 in the CEE-alone and CEE+MPA trials, respectively. Sensitivity analyses accounting for statin use mostly mitigated the temporal decrease in LDL-C in both the placebo and HT groups, particularly in the CEE-alone trial ( Appendix 21 ). Statin uptake was greater in the placebo than HT arms of both trials. 20 Regardless, the overall effect of CEE-alone and CEE+MPA on LDL-C were similar, with rGM of 0.89 (0.87–0.91) and 0.88 (0.87–90), respectively.
Adherence analyses indicated a slightly greater LDL-C reduction . The overall effects of CEE-alone and CEE+MPA on LDL-C were rGM (95%CI) = 0.87 (0.85–0.89) and rGM (95%CI) =0.85 (0.84–0.87), respectively ( Appendix 22 ). Discussion:
This study found that participants in the CEE and CEE+MPA trials had relatively favorable long-term e changes to most CVD biomarkers, including LDL-C, HDL-C, Lp(a), total cholesterol, glucose, insulin, fibrinogen and HOMA-IR when randomized to HT. However, both formulations of HT increased triglycerides and factor VII antigen. Whereas CEE trial participants had a more favorable improvement on HDL-C than CEE+MPA, there was no difference in effects on LDL-C between CEE and CEE+MPA. While both formulations significantly decreased Lp(a) concentrations, this was most pronounced among those who self-reported as American Indian or Alaska Native or Asian or Pacific Islander. The favorable influence on all CVD-biomarkers, except for triglycerides, was similar across 10-year age groups for both HT trials. This finding is surprising as HT had more adverse cardiovascular effects in older compared to younger menopausal women. While menopausal symptom severity has been shown to associate with CVD, 23 , 24 the biomarker changes inCEE and CEE+MPA trials did not differ by vasomotor symptom severity.
Our results must be considered in the context of the original WHI-HT trials which found no benefit of CEE+MPA or CEE alone on the primary outcome of coronary heart disease (CHD) events (hazard ratio [HR] =1.18, 95% CI 0.95–1.45 and HR=0.94, 95% CI 0.78–1.14, respectively) and an increased risk of stroke. 25 , 26 Oral HT increases C-reactive protein, through first pass metabolism in the liver 27 , 28 and additional markers of inflammation 29 which have been shown to increase the risk of cardiovascular events in women. 30 Also, HT increases prothrombin concentrations, and other markers of coagulation 31 in addition to raising triglycerides 1 which may counterbalance the beneficial long-term effects observed on LDL-C, HDL-C, Lp(a), insulin and glucose suggested in this study. In both WHI-HT trials, there was an early increase in CHD after randomization to HT that attenuated later in follow-up. One potential explanation is that oral HT results in early harmful prothrombotic effects on existing subclinical plaques, resulting in an increased risk of CHD events. The long-term beneficial effects of HT on lipids and glucose homeostasis may take longer to result in CHD benefit and in those women without underlying plaques. This was suggested in our study by the early increase in factor VII antigen concentrations with both CEE and CEE+MPA. Decreased fibrinogen level in both HT arms may be due to a complex interplay of factors, including the hormonal effects on the liver, changes in clotting and fibrinolytic factors, and individual responses to HT. 32 , 33 Another potential explanation for the discrepant results and area of future research is HT’s lack of an effect on LDL particle concentration, which is associated with CHD risk in women. 34 In a case-control sub-study of the WHI HT trials, women with early CHD events and matched controls had lipid subclasses measured at baseline and year one following randomization to HT (CEE and CEE+MPA). Despite lowering LDL-C, these treatments did not reduce LDL particle concentrations. 26
35
The route of estrogen delivery and type of progestogen may have different effects on biomarkers. The Kronos Early Estrogen Prevention Study (KEEPS) tested oral low-dose CEE or transdermal 17β-estradiol with 12 days per month of oral progesterone versus placebo in 727 healthy, recently menopausal women. . 2 After four years, low-dose CEE reduced LDL-C and total cholesterol with increased HDL-C and triglycerides. Transdermal 17β-estradiol reduced total cholesterol, insulin, and HOMA-IR. Notably, transdermal estrogen did not result in an increase in triglycerides compared to placebo. 2 A review of 42 different HT formulations founds that all estrogens lowered total cholesterol and LDL while raising HDL. Oral estrogen increased triglycerides while transdermal estrogen decreased triglycerides. Estrogen’s increase in HDL and triglycerides was attenuated the least by dydrogesterone and medrogestone, followed by progesterone, cyproterone acetate, and medroxyprogesterone acetate.
Our Lp(a) findings are intriguing and warrant further study. Elevated Lp(a) concentrations are considered a risk-enhancing factor for developing atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) and can indicate the need to intensify ASCVD preventive therapies such as lipid-lowering therapy. 36 , 37 Small studies with short follow-up durations have reported modest Lp(a) reductions with HT 38 , 39 , and an observational study found that HT attenuated the increased risk of CVD associated with higher Lp(a) concentrations. 40 In our study, the 35.0% to 40.0% reduction in participants with American Indian or Alaska Native ancestry or Asian or Pacific Islander provides compelling discovery-validation evidence. Lifestyle changes and statins are ineffective at lowering Lp(a), and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors provide a modest reduction of less than 30%. 41 Lp(a) is generally accepted as a familial risk factor for CVD and interactions with self-reported race may be related to a combination of socioeconomic and behavioral factors. 42
This study has limitations which limit generalizability, as WHI tested oral CEE and CEE+MPA. Different formulations and routes of HT administration may have different effects on cardiovascular biomarkers, as observed in the KEEPs trial. This may be due to first-pass metabolism in the liver observed with oral HT. In addition, the intervention phase of the WHI-HT trials began in the 1990s when statin use differed from contemporary utilization. In addition, women with vasomotor symptoms who were taking HT and unwilling to discontinue were excluded from the WHI-HT trials, which may limit extrapolation to typical use populations. Consequently, it would be helpful to study whether these biomarker effects in younger women, prior to potential subclinical plaque formation, would be associated with varying effects on CVD outcomes.
In conclusion, CEE alone and CEE+MPA were associated with favorable long-term effects on all cardiovascular biomarkers measured except triglycerides and factor VII antigen. CEE alone had a more favorable effect on HDL-C than CEE+MPA, but no difference in LDL-C was observed between CEE and CEE+MPA. Both formulations significantly reduced Lp(a), most pronounced among women with American Indian or Alaskan Native ancestry and Asian or Pacific Islander ancestry. These results may be used to counsel younger menopausal women with bothersome symptoms who are deciding whether to initiate oral HT within the context of published effects of oral HT on rates of cardiovascular events. Future research should assess whether other progestogen formulations are less likely to attenuate estrogen’s long-term effect on HDL-C and HT’s long-term effect on LDL particle size. Additional research is needed to explore the association between genetic differences and HT’s effect on Lp(a) in specific populations of interest. Supplementary Material
