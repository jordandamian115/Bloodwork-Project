Quick Guide
The lipoprotein a test measures Lp(a), a cholesterol-carrying particle in your blood that’s largely set by your genes. Unlike LDL cholesterol, Lp(a) doesn’t respond much to diet or exercise. High levels are linked to a greater risk of heart attack, stroke, and aortic valve disease.

About the Lipoprotein (a) Test
Purpose of the test
Lp(a) has emerged as an independent and genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor not adequately captured by conventional lipid profiling. Elevated Lp(a) levels are associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, and calcific aortic valve stenosis, and appear to be particularly relevant in the context of premature cardiovascular events.
The Lp(a) blood test checks for an inherited cardiovascular risk factor that standard cholesterol panels miss. Your provider orders it when your overall cardiovascular risk seems higher than your LDL, HDL, and triglyceride numbers explain.
Lp(a) isn’t part of a routine lipid panel. You can have perfectly normal LDL cholesterol and still carry high Lp(a). That’s why testing matters most if you or a close family member developed heart disease at a young age, or if your cardiovascular risk seems unexplained.
The test serves one clinical purpose:

Screening: The 2025 focused update to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) guidelines recommends at least one Lp(a) measurement in every adult’s lifetime, with priority for people who have a personal or family history of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), a genetic condition causing very high LDL cholesterol.

The test doesn’t diagnose heart disease on its own. It doesn’t measure LDL, HDL, triglycerides , or inflammation. Those require separate testing.
What does the lipoprotein (a) test measure?
A single blood sample is processed at a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified lab. The test covers one marker:

Lipoprotein (a) (Lp(a)): The concentration of Lp(a) particles in your blood, a genetically inherited cholesterol-carrying particle tied to cardiovascular risk.

When should I get a lipoprotein (a) test?
Consider testing if any of these apply:

A personal history of heart attack, stroke, or aortic valve disease, especially at a young age
A family history of premature cardiovascular disease in a parent or sibling
Diagnosed or suspected familial hypercholesterolemia
High LDL cholesterol that hasn’t responded as expected to statin therapy
Unexplained cardiovascular risk despite normal cholesterol results
A first-degree relative with known high Lp(a)

For routine screening, most major cardiology guidelines recommend testing for:

Adults with a personal or family history of premature ASCVD, at least once in a lifetime
Adults with familial hypercholesterolemia, as part of initial cardiovascular risk assessment
Women approaching or past menopause with a family history of cardiovascular disease, since Lp(a) can rise when estrogen drops
Anyone whose provider is reassessing cardiovascular risk before starting or adjusting cholesterol-lowering therapy

How It Works
How to get tested
You can order the Lipoprotein (a) Test, Lp(a) yourself online, with no doctor visit or referral needed. A licensed physician reviews and authorizes your order as part of the process. Most results are ready in one to three business days and appear in your secure online account.
Before the test
Because Lp(a) is genetically set, no fasting is required. Lp(a) levels aren’t affected by what you eat or drink, so you can have a normal meal beforehand. That’s different from LDL cholesterol or triglycerides, which shift with food intake.
Tell your provider about any medications and supplements you take. Some drugs can modestly affect Lp(a) levels, including niacin and PCSK9 inhibitors (a class of cholesterol-lowering medications).
If you’ve been recently ill, let your provider know. Lp(a) is also an acute-phase reactant, meaning it can rise temporarily during illness or inflammation. Bring a photo ID to your appointment. No other preparation is needed.
During the test

Check in at the patient service center or lab. A phlebotomist reviews your order and confirms your identity.
A band is tied around your upper arm to make the vein easier to find. The skin is cleaned with an antiseptic wipe.
A needle goes into a vein, usually on the inside of your elbow. A small vial of blood is collected. You’ll feel a brief pinch.
The needle is removed and a small bandage covers the site.

The whole draw takes about five minutes. Keep the bandage on for at least 15 minutes afterward. Some light bruising is normal and fades in a day or two. Call your provider if you notice lasting pain, swelling, or signs of infection.
After the test
Results are typically ready within one to three business days after the lab receives your sample, though timing can vary by lab. Your results will come in your secure online account. Ask your provider to walk you through what your number means alongside your full cardiovascular risk profile.

What Do My Results Mean?
Lp(a) is reported in one of two units: milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL) or nanomoles per liter (nmol/L). Which unit you see depends on the lab. U.S. labs often use mg/dL; international guidelines frequently use nmol/L.
The conversion between them isn’t straightforward. Lp(a) particle size varies between people, so a result in one unit can’t be reliably converted to the other with a simple formula. Check your report to see which unit was used.

Reference ranges are approximate and vary by lab. The 50 mg/dL threshold is the most widely cited clinical cutoff, with elevated Lp(a) levels associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, and calcific aortic valve stenosis. Your result matters most when read alongside your other cardiovascular risk factors.
If your results are normal
Your Lp(a) is below the high-risk threshold. That’s good news for this specific genetic risk factor. But it doesn’t wipe out cardiovascular risk from other sources. LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, and family history all still matter.
A normal Lp(a) means this particular inherited contributor isn’t a concern, not that your heart health is fully assessed.
If your results are high
Your body carries more Lp(a) than is considered low-risk. The higher the level, the greater the associated risk for coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, and aortic valve disease. Elevated serum Lp(a) is an independent predictor of coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction. This risk adds to your overall picture even if your standard lipid panel looks fine.
Here’s what makes Lp(a) different from LDL: you can’t meaningfully lower it through diet, exercise, or most standard cholesterol medications. Lp(a) is not adequately captured by conventional lipid profiling, and statins have little effect on it. The clinical response to a high result is usually to manage other risk factors more aggressively, including LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, and inflammation . Your provider may recommend more frequent cardiovascular monitoring or a referral to a cardiologist.
Emerging therapies that target Lp(a) are in late-stage clinical trials. Pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide targeting apo(a) mRNA, is being evaluated in the pivotal Phase 3 Lp(a)HORIZON trial enrolling more than 8,300 patients with established ASCVD. Olpasiran, currently in the Phase 3 OCEAN(a) trial, has completed recruitment of over 7,200 patients with ASCVD and Lp(a) at 200 nmol/L or higher, with Phase 2 results showing a 94% reduction in Lp(a). Neither has received regulatory approval as of mid-2026; Phase 3 cardiovascular outcomes data are expected in 2026, with regulatory submissions to follow if results are positive. PCSK9 inhibitors modestly reduce Lp(a) as a secondary effect.
Because Lp(a) is inherited, first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, adult children) of someone with high Lp(a) should consider getting tested too. Elevated Lp(a) is heritable, following an autosomal codominant inheritance pattern, so it can pass directly from parent to child regardless of sex.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal range for a lipoprotein (a) test?

A level below 30 mg/dL (or below 75 nmol/L) is seen as low risk. Levels above 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L) are considered high risk for cardiovascular disease. Ranges vary by lab, so check your report’s reference values.

Do you need to fast before a lipoprotein (a) test?

No. Lp(a) is genetically set, so you can eat and drink normally before the draw. This is different from LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, which do shift with meals.

How is the lipoprotein (a) test different from a regular cholesterol test?

A standard cholesterol test measures total cholesterol, LDL, HDL cholesterol , and triglycerides. Lp(a) isn’t captured by a standard lipid panel. You can have completely normal cholesterol numbers and still have high Lp(a), which is why the two tests aren’t interchangeable.

Do you only need to have the Lp(a) test done once?

Yes, in most cases. Lp(a) levels stay stable throughout adulthood. One measurement is usually enough for a lifetime. Repeat testing may be ordered if you start a therapy aimed at lowering Lp(a), or if your provider has another clinical reason.

Is lipoprotein (a) included in standard lipid panels?

No. Lp(a) must be ordered separately. That’s why many people with high Lp(a) don’t know it until they ask for this specific test.

What does a high lipoprotein (a) result mean for my family?

Because Lp(a) is inherited, a high result means your first-degree relatives may carry high levels too. Parents, siblings, and adult children are all worth testing. This is sometimes called cascade testing, and it’s one of the most useful steps you can take after getting a high result.

Can lifestyle changes lower my lipoprotein (a)?

No, not meaningfully. Diet and exercise have little effect on Lp(a) because it’s genetically set. After a high result, the focus shifts to managing risk factors you can change, like LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, and smoking.

Will anyone see my results besides me?

No. Your results go to your secure online account and are not shared with anyone else. You can download or print them to share with your own doctor if you choose.

Can I use insurance to pay for this test?

We do not bill insurance directly, which keeps your testing and results private. You can pay with HSA or FSA funds, and we can provide an itemized receipt if you want to request reimbursement.
