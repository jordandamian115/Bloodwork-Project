Abstract
Background
Biochemical substances relating to renal and hepatic function are influenced not only by individual factors such as gender, lifestyle, and age but also by ecological factors, such as altitude, climate, and ethnic background. The purpose of the present study was to establish reference intervals for 16 biochemical substances relating to renal and hepatic function in healthy Chinese adults. Methods
A total of 2,405 apparently healthy individuals (18–77 years of age) were chosen as reference individuals in the present study. The 16 biochemical substances relating to renal and hepatic function were analyzed using a HITACHI RL7600 analyzer. The reference intervals were established using nonparametric methods to estimate the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of the distribution as the lower and the upper limits, respectively. Results
Age‐ and gender‐appropriate reference intervals were established for some biochemical substances relating to renal and hepatic function in healthy Chinese adults. Conclusion
The reference intervals established in this study can provide a useful clinical tool for the assessment of the kidney and liver damage. In addition, the established reference intervals can be adopted in other clinical laboratories after further validation. Keywords: Reference values, Kidney function tests, Liver function tests INTRODUCTION
Reference intervals are used by the clinician as an important tool to differentiate between healthy and diseased populations 1 , 2 . Kidney and liver function tests can be useful to determine whether the kidney and liver are performing their tasks adequately. Therefore, these tests are important items in occupational health examinations. Many clinical laboratories are either using the manufacturers’ reference intervals or those published in textbooks without further validation. As we know, many reference intervals provided by manufacturers are derived from laboratory data on foreign populations. However, biochemical substances relating to renal and hepatic function are influenced not only by individual factors such as gender, lifestyle, and age but also by ecological factors, such as altitude, climate, and ethnic background 3 , 4 , 5 . In addition, differences can be found between repeated tests on the same person due to different experimental methods, different experimental analyzers, different laboratory conditions, and intra‐individual variations 6 , 7 , 8 . Appropriate reference intervals for adults will help clinical physicians make an early diagnosis and decide whether medical intervention is required. Therefore, it is essential to establish reference intervals using the native population. Reference intervals for populations from the Western countries are widely published in the textbooks 9 , 10 and scientific literature 11 . However, there is a general lack of published reference intervals for populations living in China. Although a study by Chan et al. 12 reported the reference intervals of common serum analytes for adults living in Hong Kong, reference intervals for adults living in Shanghai City are rarely reported.
In this study, we report reference intervals for 16 biochemical substances related to renal and hepatic function using 2,405 apparently healthy Chinese adults according to the recommendations of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) 1 , 8 , 13 , 14 , 15 and Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 3 . MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study Population
The reference sample group started with 3,615 adult individuals (18–77 years of age) from eastern China, covering cities and villages. The population studied didn not consist of migrant workers. Informed written consent was obtained from all of the subjects. Information regarding age, gender, environment, dietary habits, smoking or alcoholic habit, drugs, physical activity, ethnic background, disease if any, anthropometric parameters, and career were obtained in respect to each subject. To select apparently healthy persons, the physical health status was assessed by a physical examination and some clinical laboratory assays, including those for glucose, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) exposure to HIV or hepatitis; (b) hypertension, chronic renal failure, diabetes mellitus, or other diseases that could affect the values of the biochemical substances measured in this study; (c) had taken drugs within 2 weeks, undergone surgery during the past 6 months; (d) pregnant women; (e) excessive smoking (12 cigarettes/day) and drinking (30 g/day); (f) obesity (body mass index ≥ 30); (g) excessive exercise; (h) fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/l; (i) the results of glucose, bilirubin, and protein in urine were abnormal. A total of 1,201 individuals were excluded and finally 2,414 apparently healthy adults were included in this study. The median body mass was 23.12 kg/m 2 in male and 22.03 kg/m 2 in female. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People's Hospital. Specimen Collection and Handing
All blood specimens were collected in untreated vacuum tubes after an overnight fast. All blood specimens were left at room temperature (25°C) for 30 min to clot. Next, the blood in the vacuum tubes was centrifuged for 10 min at 1,200 × g . The values of the following analytes in serum collected were measured with the HITACHI RL7600 analyzer using the manufacturer's reagents: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bile acid (TBA), cholinesterase (ChE), gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), prealbumin (PA), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cre), uric acid (UA), retinol‐binding protein (RBP), and cystatin‐C (Cys‐C). All analytes were measured within 2 h. Table  1 summarizes the test methods for each analyte. Inter‐ and intraassay coefficients of variation for each analyte obtained from the manufacturer also summarized in Table  1 . A calibration procedure was carried out using multicalibrator serum to ensure accuracy and precision. Commercial quality control materials at concentrations within and outside the reference intervals were measured at least once every day. All the daily quality control runs were within ± 2 SD from their target values.

CV, coefficients of variation. Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (Version 17.0, SPSS Inc. Chicago, Illinois, USA). Prior to the reference interval calculations, the results from all of the subjects were divided into gender for male and female, and age ranging from 18 to 27, 28 to 37, 38 to 47, 48 to 57, 58 to 67, and 68 to 78 years old. Outliers were removed from each group when the observed value of D was over one‐third of the R according to Dixon's test 17 , where D is the absolute difference between the largest (or smallest) value and the next largest (or smallest) value, and R is the range of all observations. After the exclusion of obvious observed outliers, we used the normal deviate test to decide whether the separate reference intervals will be essential for the defined subgroups of subjects according to the Harris and Boyd method 16 .
The following formula was used to calculate the Z‐values

Z = x 1 ¯ − x 2 ¯ s 1 2 n 1 + s 2 2 n 2
and then the calculated statistic Z‐values were compared with the critical values calculated as,

Z * = 3 n 1 + n 2 240 s i
where χ i , s i , and n i are the mean, standard deviation, and the sample size of subgroup i , respectively. If the calculated Z value exceeds the critical value, then separate reference intervals should be established for each subgroup. Reference intervals were established using a nonparametric method to estimate 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of distribution as the lower and the upper limits, respectively. Differences in medians were assessed by the Mann–Whitney U test. The Spearman correlation was used to test the relationship between the body mass index and each analyte. A value of P < 0.05 was considered significantly different. RESULTS
Seven outliers were detected in ALT, AST, and GGT, two outliers in ALP, and all the results for these subjects were excluded from this study. Finally, 2,405 subjects (1,256 men and 1,149 women) were selected as reference individuals. The calculated reference intervals for 16 biochemical substances are summarized in Table  2 , partitioned by age and/or gender, as per the Harris and Boyd method. Table  2 shows the established reference intervals for 16 biochemical substances and their comparison with the reference intervals currently used in our laboratory. The results for ALT, GGT, Alb, PA, UA, Cre, and RBP appear generally higher in males than in females ( P < 0.05). Necessity to partition reference intervals into gender‐specific subgroups was demonstrated statistically in ALT, GGT, Alb, PA, UA, Cre, and RBP ( Z > Z *). In addition, the groups of individuals over 68 years old had lower values of ALT, ChE, and Alb compared with the groups of individuals aged 18 to 67 years old ( P < 0.05). The groups of individuals over 28 years old had higher activities of AST and ALP compared with the groups of individuals aged 18 to 27 years old ( P <0.05). In contrast, TBA concentration was higher at the age of 68 to 77 years old ( P < 0.05). The groups of individuals aged 18 to 57 years old had lower concentrations of TBIL and DBIL compared with the groups of individuals aged 58 to 77 years old ( P < 0.05). In contrast, GGT and RBP values were higher at the age of 18 to 57 years old ( P < 0.05). PA demonstrated a decreased concentration with increasing age ( P < 0.05). However, the concentration of other analytes such as TP, UA, Cre, BUN, and Cys‐C appeared to have no differences between age groups. Necessity to partition reference intervals into gender‐specific subgroups was demonstrated statistically in ALT, AST, ChE, GGT, ALP, TBA, Alb, TBIL, DBIL, PA, and RBP ( Z > Z *). Compared with the values currently used in our laboratory, both the upper and lower limits of the study reference intervals displayed an increase in the ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, TBA, TBIL, DBIL, BUN, and Cys‐C values. However, PA, Cre, and RBP showed a decrease in both the lower and the upper limits. Cys‐C showed the maximum percentage of the study population (41.61% of the males and 36.10% of the females) outside the existing reference intervals, followed by UA (19.24% of the males and 24.13% of the females).

* P < 0.05. DISCUSSION
The interpretation of laboratory testing results depends, to a significant extent, on the use of reference intervals. However, many clinical laboratories adopt the manufacturers’ reference intervals without further validation. As we know, many reference intervals provided by manufacturers are derived from laboratory data on foreign populations. The use of inappropriate reference intervals will directly affect the clinical doctors making medical diagnoses and management decisions.
As with other clinical laboratories in China, our laboratory has used the reference intervals provided by manufacturers or published in textbooks. However, the details of the procedures used to establish the reference intervals are not mentioned. These details include the studied population, experimental method, specimen collection, etc. In addition, many reference intervals currently used are not divided into subgroups by gender or age. Our results showed that ALT, GGT, Alb, PA, UA, Cre, and RBP values appear generally higher in males than in females ( P < 0.05). The higher ALT and Cre values in men than in women may be explained by the greater muscle mass in men than in women 23 . The higher UA in men than in women may be due to the higher clearance rate in women than in men 24 . The gender difference in GGT may be due to extra production from the prostate gland in men as compared to women who have no prostate gland. Lower Alb, PA, and RBP concentrations in women compared to men indicate that the synthesis of these analytes is gender dependent. In addition, age differences in the reference intervals established were observed in ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, ChE, TBA, Alb, PA, TBIL, DBIL, and RBP in this study. Age differences were also observed for ALT, AST, ALP, Alb, GGT, TBIL, and DBIL in other studies 17 , 18 , 20 . However, age differences for PA and RBP were rarely reported in the literature. Our concern is whether the separate reference intervals will be essential for the defined subgroups of subjects. According to the Harris and Boyd's criteria, if the calculated Z value exceeds the critical value, then partitioning the reference intervals into age‐ or gender‐specific was necessary.
In establishing reference intervals, completely “healthy” adults‐based values are optimal. Nevertheless, “health” is a relative concept lacking a universal definition 25 . In this study, each of the subjects underwent an overall physical examination with complete histories. In sampling, we also applied strict exclusion criteria. Last, a total of 2,405 apparently healthy adults aged 18 to 77 years (1,256 men and 1149 women) were selected randomly from the Sixth People's Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University. With this carefully selected reference sample group, we reported reference intervals for 16 biochemical substances for apparently healthy adults from eastern China. The reference intervals established in this study differ, to some extent, from those published in the literature 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 . The differences can be explained by the differences in the experimental methods, laboratory conditions, geographical locations, sample sizes, races, etc.
We acknowledge that there is at least one limitation in the present work. The mean values of the 16 biochemical substances in healthy adults are not compared with those in children.
In summary, we reported reference intervals for biochemical substances related to renal and hepatic function for apparently healthy adults from eastern China. The reference intervals established in this study can serve as a useful clinical tool for the assessment of kidney and liver damage. In addition, the reference intervals can be adopted in other clinical laboratories after local validation. REFERENCES
